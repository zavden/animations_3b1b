Harry Potter was a highly unusual boy, at least according to everyone who lived on his street, and he had long since stopped arguing with them about it. Summer had arrived early that year, heavy and still, and the hedges along the road had been trimmed so neatly that they looked painted onto the pavement. Every morning the same car pulled out of the same driveway at the same minute, and every evening it returned, and in between nothing whatsoever seemed to happen.
Inside the house, he kept his books under a loose board beneath the bed, where nobody would think to look. He read them late at night by the light of a small torch, turning the pages slowly so the paper would not rustle. There was an essay due when term began, a long one, about a subject he found both fascinating and slightly ridiculous, and he had promised himself he would finish it before the end of July.
His aunt had other plans for his summer. There were windows to wash, a garden shed to sort, and an entire attic of boxes that apparently could not survive another year without being labelled. He carried the boxes down the narrow ladder one by one, sneezing at the dust, and stacked them in the hall according to a system that changed every afternoon. Some held old curtains, some held cracked plates, and one, which he was told never to open, rattled in a way that made the cat leave the room. In the evenings his cousin watched television at a volume that made the glasses in the cupboard hum, and his uncle read the newspaper aloud, complaining about every single article in turn, from the price of petrol to the weather in countries he had never visited and never intended to.
The essay did not cooperate. Each paragraph seemed to wander off in a direction of its own, and every time he crossed out a sentence, two more appeared to take its place. He wrote about candles that refused to stay lit, about old rules that nobody remembered making, and about the strange habit grown people had of insisting that nothing interesting had ever happened to them.
Outside, the street lamps buzzed and the moths circled them in patient loops. Somewhere a dog barked three times and then thought better of it. He put down his pen, rubbed his eyes, and counted the days until he could leave again: thirty one, if he did not count today, which he decided he would not.
The letters arrived the next morning, all at once, tapping at the window before the sun was properly up. One was from a friend who wrote mostly in capital letters, one was from a friend who wrote in very small and very tidy handwriting, and one was a printed list of the books he would need for the coming year. He read them all twice, grinning, and hid them with the others under the board.
By the afternoon the house had returned to its usual silence. He sat at the desk by the window and looked again at the unfinished essay, then at the list of books, then at the timetable that had come folded inside the last envelope. Most of it was exactly what he had expected.
